Removing an Old Newspaper Article from Google in Italy: What Actually Works

By RMA — Web Reputation Agency

An old or damaging newspaper article can usually be dealt with in Italy, but rarely by making the story vanish from the internet. The routes that work are asking the publisher to update, anonymise or deindex the page, sending a formal correction (rettifica) when facts are wrong, and filing a right-to-be-forgotten request so that Google stops showing the article for searches on your name. The right choice depends on whether the article is false, outdated or simply unwelcome.

Can you really remove news articles from Google?

Yes, but what you remove is visibility, not history. Google only indexes a page that sits on the newspaper's server. That means there are always two separate targets: the page itself, which only the publisher controls, and the search result, which Google controls.

When the publisher deletes or blocks the page, Google drops it after the next crawl and the problem is solved at the source. When only Google delists the result, the article stays on the newspaper's website, in its internal search engine and in Google results for queries that do not contain your name. Both outcomes are useful, but they are not the same thing, and it pays to understand the difference between removal, deindexing and suppression before writing a single letter.

Start with the publisher: update, anonymise or deindex

The publisher is the most powerful lever, because only the newspaper can change the page itself. Italian editors rarely delete archived articles, but they often accept softer measures: an update reporting the outcome of the case, initials instead of the full name, a removed photo, or a noindex tag that drops the page from search while the archive stays intact.

These requests have a solid basis. Italian case law holds that an online news archive must be kept accurate and contextualised, and the journalists' deontological rules on personal data require that details be essential to the story. A newspaper is also a data controller under the GDPR, so the right to erasure (art. 17) and the right to object (art. 21) apply to it, balanced against freedom of expression.

A request that editors actually act on usually follows this structure:

  1. Identify the exact URL, headline and date, plus copies on the same site (tag pages, PDF print editions).
  2. Explain briefly what has changed since publication: an acquittal, a dismissed complaint, years without new developments.
  3. State the legal basis briefly: GDPR arts. 17 and 21, the Codice Privacy and the journalists' deontological rules.
  4. Ask for one specific, proportionate measure, such as noindex or anonymisation, rather than demanding deletion of the whole article.
  5. Attach supporting documents, for example the judgment or the order of dismissal, and send everything by PEC to the editor-in-chief and to the publisher's data protection officer.

Rettifica: when the article is simply wrong

If the article contains false statements, the right of correction under art. 8 of the Italian Press Law (Legge 47/1948) is the first tool to use. The director must publish your correction within the legal time limits, provided it is not itself unlawful.

A correction does not remove the article, yet it changes its weight. Readers see the corrected version, editors often amend the text, and a documented refusal becomes useful evidence for a later defamation complaint under art. 595 of the Criminal Code or for a request to Google.

The right to be forgotten: delisting accurate but outdated articles

When an article was accurate but is no longer relevant, the standard route is a request to Google under GDPR art. 17, as interpreted by the Court of Justice in Google Spain (C-131/12). Google is asked to stop showing the URL for searches containing your name across its European services; the article itself is not touched. This is the main legal path to remove old news articles from Google when the newspaper refuses to act.

Google reviews each URL individually and balances your privacy against the public's interest in information. The factors that carry most weight are:

  • how much time has passed since the events and whether anything new has happened;
  • your role in public life, since politicians and senior executives face a stricter test than private individuals;
  • the nature of the information, with criminal and health data treated as particularly sensitive;
  • the outcome of any proceedings, especially acquittal or dismissal;

For people involved in criminal proceedings that ended well, the Riforma Cartabia added art. 64-ter to the implementing provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure. After an acquittal, a dismissal or a decision not to proceed, you can ask the court registry to annotate the decision with a formal instruction that supports deindexing, which then accompanies your requests to search engines and publishers. If Google still refuses, the next steps are a complaint (reclamo) to the Garante per la protezione dei dati personali or proceedings before an Italian court. When the page is gone but Google still shows it

If the publisher has already deleted the article or edited out your name, but the old title and snippet still appear in search, use Google's outdated content tool in the Search Console Removals section. It does not require owning the site and works when the page returns a 404 or 410 error or no longer contains what the snippet shows.

Copies are the second part of this clean-up. Popular Italian stories are republished by aggregators, local portals and scraper sites, each a separate page with its own owner. To remove negative news from Google completely, every copy has to be identified and handled on its own terms, and some will need a fresh right-to-be-forgotten request. What a news article removal service can and cannot do

A professional news article removal service saves time mainly through diagnosis and persistence. It maps every result that mentions you, separates the articles that can be corrected, anonymised or delisted from those that cannot, drafts requests in Italian with the correct legal references, and follows up with editors and with Google until each case is closed.

Equally important is knowing when removal is not realistic. A recent, accurate article about a public figure on a matter of public interest will usually stay, and no legitimate provider can promise otherwise. Then the work shifts to a factual response and to building accurate results that move the article down page one. Be wary of anyone who guarantees results or suggests fake copyright notices: such methods create legal risk and often make the story bigger.

The fastest way to remove news articles from Google is rarely the most aggressive one. A documented, proportionate request to the right recipient, publisher first and Google second, solves more cases than any legal threat.
