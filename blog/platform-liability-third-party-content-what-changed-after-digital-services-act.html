Platform Liability for Third-Party Content: What Changed After the Digital Services Act

By RMA — Web Reputation Agency

The legal framework governing how online platforms handle content created by users has undergone a substantial revision across the European Union. The Digital Services Act establishes clearer rules on piattaforma responsabilità, defines structured procedures for addressing illegal material, and strengthens mechanisms that support legal reputation protection. For individuals and organisations in Italy dealing with diffamazione online or the broader need for rimozione contenuti online, the regulation supplies practical tools that were less formalised under earlier rules.

This article examines the architecture of the Digital Services Act, the revised approach to intermediary liability, the concrete stages involved in using the notice-and-action system, the categories of platforms subject to heightened duties, and the residual limitations that still apply. The focus remains on accurate description of procedures, requirements, and effects rather than promotional claims.

Core Architecture of the Digital Services Act

The Digital Services Act is an EU regulation that applies uniformly in all member states, including Italy. It builds on the earlier e-commerce directive while introducing more detailed operational duties for intermediary services. The central principle of limited intermediary liability is retained: platforms are not automatically responsible for every item of third-party content. At the same time the regulation imposes layered due diligence obligations that scale according to the size and societal impact of the service.

Services are classified into several tiers. The basic rules apply to all intermediary services. Hosting providers face additional notice-and-action requirements. Very Large Online Platforms and Very Large Online Search Engines—those reaching more than 45 million average monthly active users in the Union—must comply with the most extensive set of obligations. These include systemic risk assessments, independent audits, and detailed transparency reports.

The regulation distinguishes between illegal content and content that may be harmful but legal. Platforms are required to act against the former once they obtain actual knowledge. For the latter they retain greater discretion under their own terms of service. The Digital Markets Act operates in parallel and addresses competition and gatekeeper issues, yet the Digital Services Act remains the primary instrument for content-related platform accountability and content moderation obligations.

Enforcement DSA is coordinated between the European Commission and national Digital Services Coordinators. In Italy the competent authority works with the Commission to monitor compliance and to handle complaints. Significant financial penalties can be imposed for systemic failures to meet the due diligence obligations.

Key Procedural Concepts Introduced by the Regulation

Several operational concepts are central to the functioning of the Digital Services Act:

  • Notice-and-action mechanisms that allow any individual or entity to notify a platform of specific illegal content.
  • Trusted flaggers—entities that have demonstrated particular expertise and reliability and therefore receive priority treatment of their notices.
  • The right to explanation DSA, requiring platforms to provide clear reasons when content is restricted or removed.
  • Internal complaint-handling systems and access to out-of-court dispute settlement bodies.
  • Periodic transparency reports that disclose volumes of notices, actions taken, and average response times.

These elements collectively support faster takedown DSA processes when the legal threshold is met and improve the ability of affected parties to track and challenge decisions.

Revised Approach to Platform Liability

Under the previous regime platforms could rely on broad safe-harbour protections provided they remained passive and removed illegal material once they obtained knowledge of it. The Digital Services Act preserves the core safe-harbour principle while attaching clearer conditions to its continued availability. Failure to maintain effective notice-and-action systems or to act on valid notices can undermine the liability shield and expose the platform to regulatory sanctions.

Actual knowledge is typically established through a formal notice that identifies the content with sufficient precision and explains why it is illegal under applicable law. Once knowledge exists, the platform is expected to act without undue delay. The precise meaning of “without undue delay” depends on the nature of the content and the circumstances, yet the existence of a documented notice creates a clear starting point for assessing the platform’s response.

Very Large Online Platforms face additional duties linked to systemic risk. They must analyse the potential for their services to contribute to the dissemination of illegal content, to negative effects on fundamental rights, or to coordinated harmful campaigns. Mitigation measures derived from these assessments are subject to independent scrutiny. This layer of platform accountability is intended to address risks that arise from the sheer scale of the service rather than from isolated items of content.

In the Italian legal environment the Digital Services Act interacts with national rules on civil and criminal defamation. Diffamazione online continues to be assessed under Italian substantive law. The DSA supplies the procedural channel through which a finding of illegality can be communicated to the platform and through which the platform’s subsequent conduct can be evaluated. A notice that correctly references both the national legal basis and the DSA notice-and-action framework therefore carries greater weight than an informal request.

Practical Effects on Content Removal Processes

The regulation does not guarantee removal in every case. It does, however, create more predictable pathways and stronger documentation when the content in question qualifies as illegal. Businesses and individuals seeking online defamation removal or the broader remove harmful content objective can now rely on standardised forms, clearer timelines, and formal explanation requirements.

Structured Notice Submission

Most major platforms maintain dedicated channels for receiving notices of illegal content. A valid notice generally requires:

  • Precise identification of the content (URL, unique identifier, or other locator).
  • A clear explanation of the legal basis on which the content is claimed to be illegal.
  • Contact details of the notifier sufficient to allow the platform to communicate a decision.
  • Where relevant, supporting material that substantiates the claim.

When the notifier holds trusted-flagger status the platform is expected to prioritise the notice. Even without that status a complete and accurate notice places the platform under a formal duty to assess and respond.

Response and Explanation Duties

After receiving a valid notice the platform examines the material and decides whether to remove, restrict, or retain it. The decision must be accompanied by a statement of reasons. This right to explanation DSA enables the notifier and the content provider to understand the basis of the action and to decide whether to challenge it. Internal complaint mechanisms must be available, and further recourse to certified out-of-court dispute settlement bodies is provided for under the regulation.

Transparency reports published by Very Large Online Platforms supply aggregate data on how notices are handled. These reports can be consulted when assessing the typical performance of a given service and when preparing more complex or repeated requests.

Platforms Subject to Heightened Obligations

The intensity of content moderation obligations increases with the reach of the service. Very Large Online Platforms and Very Large Online Search Engines must perform continuous systemic risk assessments, implement mitigation measures, undergo independent audits, and publish detailed transparency reports. Designation is formal and is published by the European Commission.

Large social networks, major video-sharing services, and dominant online marketplaces typically fall into this category. Smaller hosting providers and online platforms remain subject to the core notice-and-action and transparency rules but are not required to conduct the same level of systemic risk analysis. For practical purposes the distinction matters because the largest services process the highest volumes of potentially damaging material and maintain the most formalised reporting channels.

In the Italian market the majority of high-visibility reputation issues arise on services that have been designated as Very Large Online Platforms. Their enhanced duties create both higher procedural expectations and more extensive public documentation that can be referenced when evaluating compliance.

Detailed Stages of Using the DSA for Reputation-Related Content

Applying the Digital Services Act to a concrete case of alleged illegal content involves a sequence of analytical and procedural steps. Each stage has a defined purpose, requires specific inputs, and produces outputs that feed the next stage.

Stage 1: Legal Characterisation of the Content

The first task is to determine whether the material in question qualifies as illegal content under applicable law. In the Italian context this usually involves an assessment under the civil or criminal rules on defamation, together with any other relevant provisions (for example, data-protection rules or intellectual-property rules). The analysis examines the precise wording, the context, the identity of the publisher, and the available evidence of falsity or unlawfulness.

Only content that crosses the threshold of illegality triggers the platform’s duty to act under the DSA notice-and-action framework. Material that is merely critical, unpleasant, or commercially damaging but remains within the bounds of lawful expression is treated as harmful but legal. Platforms retain discretion over such material under their own policies; the Digital Services Act does not compel its removal.

Stage 2: Preparation of the Formal Notice

Once illegality is established, a formal notice is drafted. The notice must identify the content with sufficient precision so that the platform can locate it without extensive additional research. It must state the legal grounds on which the content is claimed to be illegal and must include the notifier’s contact details. Supporting documentation—court decisions, documentary evidence, or expert statements—may be attached where it strengthens the claim.

The notice should be drafted in a language accepted by the platform (often English or the language of the member state) and should avoid exaggerated or unsupported assertions. Accuracy and completeness increase the likelihood of a substantive response and create a clear record for any subsequent challenge.

Stage 3: Submission Through the Designated Channel

The completed notice is submitted through the platform’s official notice-and-action channel. Many services provide web forms, dedicated email addresses, or API-based reporting tools. When the notifier qualifies as a trusted flagger the submission is routed through the priority channel established for such entities. A confirmation of receipt is normally generated and should be retained.

At this point the platform is considered to have been placed on formal notice. The clock for assessing “undue delay” begins to run from the moment a complete notice is received.

Stage 4: Platform Assessment and Decision

The platform examines the notice and the contested content. It may request clarification if the notice is incomplete. Once the assessment is finished the platform issues a decision—removal, restriction of visibility, or retention—and supplies a statement of reasons. The decision and the accompanying explanation are communicated to the notifier and, where applicable, to the content provider.

Stage 5: Internal Complaint and Further Challenge

If the decision is considered incorrect, the internal complaint mechanism can be activated. The complaint must be lodged within the time limits set by the platform’s procedures. Should the internal review prove unsatisfactory, the matter may be referred to a certified out-of-court dispute settlement body. In parallel, regulatory complaints may be filed with the national Digital Services Coordinator or, in cases of systemic non-compliance, with the European Commission. National court proceedings remain available where the substantive rights of the affected party so require.

Each of these stages produces documentary records that can be used in subsequent legal or regulatory steps. Maintaining an organised file of notices, confirmations, decisions, and correspondence is therefore an integral part of the overall process.

Interaction with National Law and Parallel Proceedings

The Digital Services Act does not replace Italian substantive rules on defamation or other civil wrongs. It supplies a procedural interface between those rules and the operational systems of online platforms. In many cases a DSA notice is used alongside, or as a preliminary step to, civil or criminal proceedings before Italian courts. A successful removal under the DSA may reduce the practical urgency of court action; an unsuccessful notice may supply evidence of the platform’s knowledge and conduct that becomes relevant in later litigation.

Coordination between the DSA procedure and national proceedings requires careful timing and consistent legal characterisation of the content. Conflicting descriptions of the same material in different forums can weaken both tracks.

Remaining Limitations and Practical Constraints

Several constraints continue to limit the reach of the Digital Services Act. Content that is harmful but legal falls outside the mandatory removal obligation. Platforms are not required to monitor their services proactively for illegal material; knowledge must still be established, normally through a formal notice. Response times, while generally improved, are not instantaneous and can vary according to the complexity of the case and the volume of notices the platform is handling.

Cross-border elements, anonymous publishers, and incomplete identifying information can further complicate enforcement. Regulatory enforcement against non-compliant platforms is real but operates on a longer timescale than individual notice procedures. Finally, the quality of the initial legal analysis remains decisive: a notice based on an incorrect characterisation of the content is unlikely to succeed and may consume time that could have been used on more effective measures.

These limitations do not negate the value of the regulation. They simply define the boundaries within which the notice-and-action system and the associated platform accountability mechanisms operate.

Summary of the Current Framework

The Digital Services Act has introduced a more structured and transparent system for addressing illegal content on online platforms. By formalising notice-and-action procedures, imposing scaled due diligence obligations, requiring explanations of decisions, and enhancing transparency reports, the regulation improves the practical ability of affected parties to seek rimozione contenuti online and to pursue legal reputation protection. The framework is particularly relevant for cases of diffamazione online and other forms of illegal material that meet the applicable legal thresholds.

Effective use of the system depends on accurate legal characterisation, complete and precise notices, correct use of the designated channels, and realistic expectations about timelines and outcomes. When these conditions are met, the Digital Services Act supplies a clearer and more accountable pathway than the less formalised arrangements that preceded it.

For concrete cases arising in Italy or elsewhere in the European Union, a methodical application of the stages described above—beginning with careful legal assessment and proceeding through formal notice, decision, and any necessary challenge—offers the most reliable way to engage the current rules on platform liability and content moderation.
