Right to be Forgotten in the EU: What Can Really Be Removed from Google

By RMA — Web Reputation Agency

Individuals and professionals in Italy frequently encounter outdated or disproportionate information about themselves that continues to appear in search results years after the underlying events. The diritto all'oblio, known internationally as the right to be forgotten, provides a structured legal mechanism to request that search engines stop displaying certain links when a person’s name is searched. This article explains in detail what the right covers under European law, which categories of information can realistically be addressed, how each stage of the process works, and what practical outcomes can be expected.

What is diritto all’oblio / right to be forgotten

The diritto all'oblio is the right of a natural person to ask a search engine to remove or stop listing links to personal information that is no longer relevant, accurate or proportionate. It does not delete the original web page. The content remains available if someone knows the exact URL or finds it through other means. What changes is the visibility in search results for queries that include the individual’s name. In practice this significantly reduces the chance that ordinary users will encounter the information.

The concept rests on the idea that personal data should not remain indefinitely accessible simply because it was once published. When information becomes obsolete or the balance between privacy and public interest shifts, the individual can seek deindicizzazione Google so that the links no longer appear under name-based searches within the European Union. The right applies primarily to living natural persons and is exercised most often against Google because of its dominant market position in Italy.

Requests under the diritto all'oblio are assessed case by case. The search engine must weigh the individual’s interest in privacy online against the public’s interest in accessing the information and the publisher’s freedom of expression. Private individuals generally receive stronger protection than public figures. Time elapsed since publication, the sensitivity of the data and the accuracy of the content all influence the outcome.

In everyday use the right to be forgotten functions as a targeted tool for managing online reputation rather than as a complete erasure of history. Many successful cases involve old criminal reports that ended without conviction, resolved financial difficulties, or personal details that no longer reflect the person’s current situation. The mechanism is available to any persona fisica who can demonstrate that the continued display of the links is no longer justified.

Legal basis in the EU (GDPR)

The principal legal foundation is the General Data Protection Regulation, in particular Article 17 on the right to erasure, also referred to as the right to be forgotten. Under GDPR a data subject may request the erasure of personal data when the data are no longer necessary for the purpose for which they were collected, when consent is withdrawn, when the processing is unlawful, or when erasure is required by law. Search engines process personal data by collecting, indexing and displaying links associated with a person’s name, and therefore fall under these obligations.

The Corte di Giustizia UE clarified the application of these rules to search engines in a landmark judgment. The Court held that a search engine operator must examine each request and decide whether continued display of the link remains justified. Relevant factors include the nature of the information, its sensitivity, the time that has passed and the role the person plays in public life. The decision established that the GDPR right to be forgotten can be exercised directly against the search engine without first approaching every original publisher.

Italian supervisory authorities and courts apply the same framework. When a request is refused, the individual may lodge a complaint with the Garante per la protezione dei dati personali or bring a civil action. In practice a well-documented richiesta diritto all'oblio often leads to a decision at the search-engine level. The regulation also requires the operator to inform the requester of the outcome and of the right to complain to a supervisory authority, ensuring procedural transparency.

The diritto alla cancellazione dati under GDPR is not absolute. It must always be balanced against other fundamental rights. This bilanciamento interessi is the core of every assessment and explains why outcomes vary from case to case.

What data can be deleted

Not every piece of information that appears in search results qualifies for removal. The assessment is individual and depends on concrete circumstances. Successful requests typically concern data that have become outdated, inaccurate or disproportionate relative to any remaining public interest.

Personal data

Core personal data form the most straightforward category for rimozione dati personali. This includes residential addresses, telephone numbers, email addresses, identity-document numbers and similar identifiers that appear on old web pages, directories or archived profiles. When such data no longer serve a legitimate purpose and their continued display creates risks of unwanted contact, identity misuse or other harm, search engines frequently accept the request.

Outdated professional information is another common subject. Old curriculum-vitae entries, former employment details or contact data that the individual no longer wishes to see associated with their name can often be deindexed once their relevance has expired. Financial information that has lost its accuracy — for example details of past payment defaults or insolvency proceedings that have been fully resolved — also frequently meets the criteria, provided sufficient time has passed and supporting documentation is supplied.

Sensitive categories such as health-related data receive heightened protection under GDPR. When medical information appears in search results without ongoing justification, the likelihood of a positive decision increases, especially if the individual can demonstrate potential harm from continued visibility.

Old news and articles

News articles and press reports require a more careful balance. Search engines do not automatically remove journalistic content. However, when an article concerns a private individual, reports events that occurred many years earlier and no longer contributes meaningfully to public debate, a request for deindicizzazione diritto all'oblio can succeed. Typical examples include reports of minor criminal proceedings that ended in acquittal or dismissal, or coverage of personal disputes that have long been resolved.

The passage of time is a decisive factor. An article published fifteen or twenty years ago about a matter with no ongoing public relevance stands a better chance than a recent report. The role of the person also matters: private citizens enjoy broader protection than politicians, business leaders or other figures whose activities remain of legitimate public interest. Even when the original article stays online, removal from name-based search results under the Google right to be forgotten significantly reduces its practical impact, because most users rarely look beyond the first page of results.

In some cases only certain links are removed while others remain. The search engine may decide that one report is still justified while another, covering the same events in a more intrusive way, is not. This selective approach reflects the required bilanciamento interessi.

What is almost impossible to delete

Certain categories of information are rarely removed. Content that remains of clear public interest — for example reporting on current or recent criminal convictions of public figures, ongoing legal proceedings, or matters involving the exercise of official authority — almost always stays visible. Information published by public authorities in the exercise of their official functions is also difficult to challenge successfully.

Recent publications generally face a higher threshold. The shorter the time that has elapsed, the stronger the public-interest argument tends to be. Content that the individual themselves made public, or that was published with their consent and has not become outdated, is likewise hard to remove. Finally, information that is still accurate and necessary for the purposes for which it was originally processed will normally remain indexed.

Attempts to use the right to be forgotten to suppress legitimate criticism, consumer reviews or accurate reporting of professional misconduct usually fail. The mechanism is designed to protect privacy online against disproportionate or obsolete information, not to rewrite history or eliminate accountability.

How to submit a right to be forgotten request

Exercising the diritto all'oblio involves a sequence of concrete steps. Each stage has its own requirements, documentation needs and possible outcomes. Understanding the process in detail helps avoid common mistakes that lead to refusal.

Through the Google form

The most direct route is the official Google form for European privacy requests. The form is available on Google’s support pages and is specifically designed for requests under the GDPR right to be forgotten. The requester must provide full name, contact details, the exact URLs of the results to be removed, and a clear explanation of why the information is no longer relevant or is causing harm.

Supporting documents strengthen the request. These may include court decisions showing that proceedings ended in acquittal, certificates confirming that debts have been settled, or evidence that the published data are inaccurate. Screenshots of the search results and copies of the original pages help the reviewer understand the context. The form also asks whether the requester is a public figure and whether the information was published by the requester themselves.

Once submitted, Google examines the request and may contact the original publishers for their views. The process is free of charge in standard cases. Response times vary, but a decision is normally issued within several weeks to a few months depending on complexity. If the request is accepted, the links are removed from search results shown to users in the European Economic Area when the person’s name is queried.

The modulo diritto all'oblio Google is the standard starting point for most individuals. It allows the requester to exercise the right to be forgotten request without immediate professional assistance, although the quality of the documentation often determines the outcome.

Through a lawyer or specialised agency

When the case is complex, involves multiple languages or jurisdictions, or has already been refused, many people turn to professional support. A specialised agenzia diritto all'oblio or a lawyer experienced in data-protection matters can prepare a more detailed submission, gather the necessary evidence, and handle correspondence with the search engine and, if required, with the supervisory authority.

Professional assistance typically includes an initial assessment of the prospects of success, identification of all relevant URLs, drafting of a reasoned request that addresses the bilanciamento interessi, and follow-up if the first decision is negative. In some situations the agency or lawyer may also approach the original publishers to request removal or anonymisation at source, which can complement the search-engine request.

Using a servizio diritto all'oblio does not guarantee success, but it increases the likelihood that the request will be complete, well-argued and properly documented. For individuals who are not familiar with GDPR procedures or who face language barriers, this route often proves more effective than a self-prepared submission.

Typical reasons for refusal

Search engines refuse requests for a limited set of recurring reasons. The most common is that the information is still considered of public interest. This applies especially to recent events, matters involving public figures, or content that relates to professional activities that remain relevant.

Another frequent ground is insufficient evidence that the data are inaccurate or outdated. Vague statements without supporting documents rarely succeed. Requests that seek to remove accurate reporting of criminal convictions that are still within the rehabilitation period, or information that the requester themselves made public, are also routinely rejected.

Technical defects in the request — missing URLs, unclear identification of the data subject, or failure to explain the specific harm — can lead to refusal or to a request for further information. Understanding these typical pitfalls allows the requester to address them in advance and improve the quality of the submission.

  • Information still of public interest or relating to public figures
  • Insufficient documentation of inaccuracy or obsolescence of the data
  • Content published by the data subject themselves or with their consent
  • Formal errors in the request (missing URLs, incomplete data)

Difference between the right to be forgotten and deindexing

The terms are closely related but not identical. The right to be forgotten, or diritto all'oblio, is the broader legal right under GDPR to obtain erasure or restriction of personal data in certain circumstances. When exercised against a search engine it usually results in deindexing: the search engine stops listing the specific URLs in results for name-based queries within the EU.

Deindicizzazione Google is therefore the practical technical outcome of a successful right-to-be-forgotten request directed at the search engine. The original page remains online and can still be accessed by direct link or through other search terms. Only the association between the person’s name and that page in the search index is removed for users in the relevant territory.

In some cases the individual may also seek actual deletion of the content from the original website. That requires a separate request to the publisher under the diritto alla cancellazione dati. Success depends on the publisher’s legal obligations and on whether one of the grounds in Article 17 GDPR is met. Many publishers, especially news organisations, resist complete deletion and prefer to keep the material online while accepting deindexing by search engines.

Understanding the distinction helps set realistic expectations. A successful Google right to be forgotten request improves privacy online by reducing visibility, but it does not guarantee that the information disappears from the internet entirely. Complementary actions against the original source may be necessary when full removal is the goal.

How much it costs and how long it takes

Submitting a standard request through the official Google form is free of charge. Google does not require payment for examining a diritto all'oblio request under GDPR. If the case is particularly complex or repetitive, the regulation allows the controller to charge a reasonable fee, but this is uncommon for genuine first-time requests.

When professional support is used, costs vary according to the scope of work. An initial assessment and preparation of a single request typically involves a fixed or hourly fee that depends on the number of URLs, the need for document collection, and whether further action before the supervisory authority is required. More extensive assistenza diritto all'oblio that includes multiple search engines, publisher contacts and possible litigation will naturally cost more.

Time frames also vary. Straightforward cases processed through the Google form often receive a decision within four to twelve weeks. Complex cases involving sensitive data, public-interest arguments or subsequent complaints to the Garante can take several months. If court proceedings become necessary, the timeline extends further according to the ordinary length of civil litigation in the relevant Italian court.

Factors that influence both cost and duration include the volume of material to be reviewed, the quality of the evidence already available, and whether the original publishers contest the request. Early preparation of clear documentation tends to shorten the overall process.

  • Self-submitted request via Google form: free of charge, average time 4–12 weeks
  • Basic professional assistance (assessment + one request): cost varies according to complexity
  • Full management including possible complaint to the Garante or litigation: longer timelines and higher costs

Real cases of successful removal

Documented outcomes illustrate the practical reach of the right. In one category of cases, individuals who had been the subject of news reports about criminal investigations that later ended in full acquittal obtained deindexing of the related articles after several years had passed and no residual public interest remained. The search engine accepted that continued display under the person’s name was no longer justified.

Another recurring group involves resolved financial difficulties. People whose names appeared in connection with past insolvency proceedings or payment defaults that had been fully settled succeeded in having the links removed once they supplied official certificates confirming the closure of the matters. The information had become inaccurate in its implication that the difficulties were ongoing.

Private individuals have also obtained removal of outdated contact details and old professional profiles that listed former addresses or telephone numbers no longer in use. In these situations the absence of any public-interest argument and the clear obsolescence of the data made the decision relatively straightforward.

Outcomes are never automatic. Each successful case rested on specific evidence showing that the information was either inaccurate, outdated or disproportionate. Cases involving public figures or recent events have a markedly lower success rate, confirming that the bilanciamento interessi remains central to every decision.

Conclusion and practical next steps

The diritto all'oblio offers a concrete legal route for individuals in Italy to reduce the visibility of obsolete or disproportionate personal information in Google search results. It does not erase the internet, but it can substantially limit the practical impact of old content on a person’s privacy online and professional life. Success depends on a careful assessment of the facts, proper documentation, and a clear explanation of why the continued display of the links is no longer justified.

Anyone considering a request should begin by identifying the exact URLs that appear under their name, gathering evidence of inaccuracy or obsolescence, and deciding whether to proceed independently through the official form or with professional support. When the material is extensive, the legal issues complex, or a previous request has been refused, specialised consulenza diritto all'oblio can help ensure that the submission meets the required standard.

The right to erasure under GDPR remains an important instrument for personal data removal in the digital environment. Used correctly, it allows individuals to restore a measure of control over how their past appears to anyone who simply types their name into a search engine.
