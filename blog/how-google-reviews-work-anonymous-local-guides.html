How Google Reviews Work: Anonymous Reviewers, Local Guides and Why Reviews Disappear

By RMA — Web Reputation Agency

Any person with a Google account can review a business on Google Maps, without proving they were ever a customer, and the owner sees only the public profile Google chooses to display. Behind every review sits an automated moderation system that can hide or remove content at any moment, which is why ratings move without anyone pressing a button. Understanding how Google reviews work is the first step before deciding whether a review can be challenged.

Who can post a review on a Google Business Profile

A review requires only a signed-in Google account; there is no purchase check, no booking reference and no receipt. That is the structural difference from platforms such as Booking.com, where only verified guests can write, and it explains why an Italian restaurant in Rome or a dental clinic in Milan can receive feedback from people who never crossed the threshold.

One account, one review per place

Each account can leave a single review for each listing. The author can edit it later, add photos, change the star rating or delete it entirely; an edited review keeps its original position in the list but shows the updated date. A star rating without any text is also accepted, and it counts towards the average exactly like a detailed review.

Conflicts of interest are banned, not blocked

Google's contribution policy prohibits reviews written by owners, employees, competitors or anyone paid or rewarded for them. The rule is enforced after the fact by detection systems, not by an identity check at the moment of posting. In Italy the same practice is also a consumer-law issue: the Codice del Consumo, as updated by the EU Omnibus Directive, treats fake or incentivised reviews as an unfair commercial practice that the AGCM can sanction.

Names, nicknames and "A Google user"

Anonymous Google reviews are anonymous only to the public, never to Google. The platform stores the account behind every contribution, including sign-in data and device signals, even when the visible name says nothing about the person.

What readers actually see depends on several factors:

  • Profile name – by default, the name set on the Google account, which can be a real name or an invented one, since Google does not verify identity for personal accounts.
  • Custom display name – Google has been introducing the option to publish contributions under a chosen nickname and avatar instead of the account name, which makes reviewers even harder to recognise.
  • "A Google user" – a generic label typically shown for older reviews or for accounts that have been deleted, suspended or restricted. The text and rating may remain online even though the author no longer has an active profile.
  • Profile photo and contribution count – visible to everyone and often the only clue to whether an account is genuine or created for a single attack.

Unmasking an anonymous reviewer is not something an owner can do through Google's interface. Where a review is defamatory under art. 595 c.p., identification requires a criminal complaint or a civil court order addressed to Google, which handles European data through Google Ireland; investigators, not the business, receive the account data.

What the Local Guide badge really means

Local Guides is a gamified loyalty programme, not a verification scheme. Users earn points for reviews, photos, answers and edits to map data, climb through ten levels and receive a visible badge from level four onwards.

A Local Guide badge therefore says the person contributes often, not that their opinion is more accurate or that they visited your premises. Their reviews pass through the same filters as anyone else's, and Google does not publicly state that they carry extra weight in the overall rating. For an Italian hotel or trattoria, a negative review from a high-level guide persuades readers more and is harder to remove, because the account history looks legitimate.

The badge measures activity, not credibility. A level-eight guide writing about a place they never visited is breaking exactly the same policy as a brand-new account.

Why Google Maps reviews disappear

Most reviews that vanish were removed by Google's automated systems, not by a person reading a complaint. Google Maps reviews are scanned when submitted and re-evaluated later, so a review that was visible for weeks can be taken down when new signals emerge.

Common filtering triggers

Detection models look at patterns rather than single words. Typical signals include a sudden burst of five-star reviews after a quiet period, several reviews sent from the same network or device as the business, accounts that review dozens of unrelated places in one day, links or phone numbers in the text, and wording copied across multiple listings. Genuine customers posting from the shop's Wi-Fi can be caught by the same net.

Deleted Google reviews that the owner did not report

Deleted Google reviews also disappear for reasons unrelated to moderation: the author removed them, closed the Google account, or the account was suspended for activity elsewhere. When listings are merged or a duplicate profile is removed, reviews attached to the suppressed version may not migrate. Under the Digital Services Act, Google now sends the author a statement of reasons when content is removed for a policy breach, and the author can appeal, so a review may also reappear after a successful challenge.

Restrictions on profiles with suspicious activity

When Google detects a coordinated fake-review campaign, it can do more than delete individual entries. In some markets it has temporarily blocked new reviews on the affected profile and displayed a warning to visitors that fake reviews were removed, a visible penalty that damages trust far more than one bad rating.

What owners can and cannot see

A business owner sees exactly what the public sees about a reviewer, plus the tools to reply and report. There is no private dashboard revealing emails, phone numbers or locations.

Turning this knowledge into decisions

Once you know how Google reviews work, the next steps for an Italian business become clearer: challenge reviews only when a specific policy is broken, protect genuine feedback from being filtered, and stop worrying about reviews that are merely harsh but lawful.

  1. Check the reviewer's public profile before reporting: a single-review account posting during a spike is a stronger case than an established local reviewer.
  2. Match the report to a named policy, such as conflict of interest, off-topic content or harassment, instead of selecting a generic reason.
  3. Ask customers to review from their own mobile data, not the shop Wi-Fi, and never offer discounts in exchange.
  4. Keep screenshots with dates of every disputed review, because evidence disappears together with the content.
